## The language that was always there

Mathematics is not just numbers, formulas, or school exercises; it is the invisible language that structures reality. From the simplest everyday decisions to the most complex systems that hold up the modern world, mathematics is constantly present, even when it goes unnoticed.

In everyday life —outside labs or academic settings— people use mathematics without realizing it: managing money, measuring ingredients, calculating time, or making decisions based on intuitive probabilities.

## Why mathematics matters

Mathematics develops a way of thinking. It's not just about calculating, but about **analyzing, abstracting, modeling, and solving problems**. According to Polya (1945), learning mathematics is deeply tied to the ability to solve problems strategically and logically.

In addition, authors like Devlin (2011) point out that mathematics is the language of patterns, allowing us to understand both natural and social phenomena.

In this sense, mathematics is key to:

- **Making better decisions:** Evaluating costs, risks, and benefits.
- **Developing critical thinking:** Analyzing information and spotting errors.
- **Understanding the world:** From natural phenomena to economic systems.
- **Creating technology:** The foundation of computing, engineering, and science.

## Mathematics in everyday life: from simple to complex

### Basic level: daily survival

The simplest math is the most used:

- **Addition and subtraction:** Managing money, calculating change on a purchase.
- **Multiplication:** Determining total costs (e.g., price times quantity).
- **Division:** Splitting resources or calculating unit prices.

Practical example:
If you buy 3 products at $2 each, you're applying basic multiplication to make a spending decision.

### Intermediate level: organization and planning

Here come concepts like proportions and percentages:

- **Percentages:** Calculating discounts, taxes, or interest.
- **Rule of three:** Adjusting recipes, budgets, or timelines.
- **Basic statistics:** Interpreting data (averages, trends).

Practical example:
If a product has a 25% discount, you're using math to evaluate whether it's actually worth buying.

### Advanced level: strategic decisions

Mathematics starts to influence more complex decisions:

- **Probability:** Assessing risk (business, investments, games).
- **Algebra:** Modeling situations (income vs. expenses).
- **Geometry:** Construction, design, and space planning.

Practical example:
An entrepreneur calculating future profits is using mathematical models, even without formalizing them.

### Complex level: the modern world

Advanced mathematics underpins today's technology and society:

- **Calculus:** Process optimization (engineering, economics).
- **Linear algebra:** The foundation of artificial intelligence and 3D graphics.
- **Advanced statistics:** Analysis of large volumes of data.

Practical example:
Every time you use social media, GPS, or digital platforms, you're interacting with systems built on complex mathematics.

## The educational disconnect

Despite its importance, many people perceive mathematics as useless or disconnected from reality. This is largely due to how it's taught.

The traditional education system tends to focus on memorizing formulas instead of practical application. As a result, students fail to connect what they learn with their everyday lives.

### Common problems:

- **Mechanical approach:** Solving exercises without understanding their purpose.
- **Lack of context:** Real applications aren't explained.
- **Math anxiety:** A perception of difficulty or uselessness.

This disconnect creates a paradox: people use math every day, but believe they don't understand it.

## A constructive critique: teaching how to think, not how to memorize

The problem isn't mathematics itself, but how it's taught. **Memorizing formulas is not learning mathematics; understanding problems is.**

Education should focus on:

- Real-world applications from early stages.
- Solving everyday problems.
- Developing logical thinking before technique.

Mathematics should be presented as a tool for understanding the world, not as an academic obstacle.

## The path toward real mathematics education

To improve how mathematics is taught, the following actions are proposed:

1. **Contextualized learning:** Connecting each topic to real situations.
2. **Focus on problem-solving:** Following methodologies like Polya's.
3. **Use of technology:** Simulations, visualizations, and digital tools.
4. **Mindset shift:** Showing that mathematics is accessible and useful.

## Conclusion

Mathematics is not just a school subject; it is a fundamental tool for life. From the simplest to the most complex, it influences our decisions, our way of thinking, and the development of society.

Understanding it is not an option reserved for experts, but an essential skill for anyone who wants to understand and transform their reality.

The real challenge isn't learning mathematics, but **learning to see it in the world around us**.
